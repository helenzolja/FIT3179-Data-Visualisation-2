Exploring Australian Native Plants

1Diversity of Australian Plants

Australia has over 20,000 native vascular plant species, roughly 10% of the global total, and almost 9 in 10 of them (88%) are endemic: found nowhere else on Earth.

Flowering plants (angiosperms) make up the vast majority of the continent's flora. Smaller groups include gymnosperms (conifers, cycads and their relatives) and bryophytes (mosses, liverworts and hornworts), small spore-producing plants that help stabilise soil.

Australia holds about 7% of the world's described flowering plants, but more than a quarter of its described plant algae.

2Plant observations in 2025

The spike map below shows the total recorded plant observations across Australia in 2025. From over 900,000 plant occurrences, the majority of them were recorded in the eastern states and along the coast, with the tallest spikes concentrated in Sydney and Melbourne.

Although a correlation can be drawn from recorded observations to plant growth density, it is important to consider that the data has a heavy "urban bias", specifically as cities have the highest density of human population meaning more concentration of citizen scientists to log sightings, as compared to regional areas.

Victoria possesses over 45 national parks and 20 prominent botanic gardens (Parks Victoria, 2026) while NSW possesses over 200 national parks and features over 20 major botanic gardens (NSW Government, 2026), all contributing to a popular hotspot for plant observation.

Fewer occurrence records across regional areas such as inner Australia could be a result of a lack of human population density and sampling bias, rather than a lack of actual plant life. Inaccessible terrain or private agricultural fields are geographically isolated, leaving vast regional ecosystems unmonitored.

3Australia's vegetation divide

Native vegetation is crucial for nourishing soils, sheltering and sustaining wildlife, protecting streams, wetlands and coastlines, as well as for cultivating our diverse flora (DCCEEW, 2021). Destruction and loss of native vegetation is a primary cause of land degradation, declining water quality, and thus the biggest cause of biodiversity loss. Although urban development and land clearing for agriculture are a big reason for this, weeds, fire spread and insect attacks are all equal threats.

As observed from the waffle chart, Australia is heavily dominated by woodlands and open forests, with tall and low shrubs following second. Prominent botanical families such as acacia and eucalyptus, which all thrive in wet and dry forests and shrublands thus dominate the landscape as a result of the vegetation divide.

4Australia's Largest Plant Families

The Myrtaceae or myrtle family is Australia's most dominant and ecologically significant plant group, containing over 1600 native species that are unique to Australia only. Some examples of species include Eucalyptus, bottlebrushes and lemon myrtles. They form the primary structural vegetation across the continent, and are majorly concentrated around the wetter rainforests.

Although it contains the largest number of genera, the Poaceae or grass family is relatively lacking in unique native species. This family occupies the ground layer, sprawling across vast arid and savanna regions.

5Native and Naturalised Plants

Source: ANBG, Australian flora statistics (1992).

Queensland and Western Australia each contain almost half (48%) of Australia's native species. Tasmania has the smallest share, at 10%.

Switch to naturalised plants (species introduced from overseas that now grow wild) and the pattern moves south-east: New South Wales holds 64% of them and Queensland 59%, while the Northern Territory has only 13%.

One likely reason is that large states span many climates, while the south-east has the longest history of European settlement, ports and farming, which are common routes for introduced plants.

6Common native plants and when and where do they grow?

This section covers the 10 most common native plants in Australia and tracks their growth over the 2025 year across seasons and states, drawn from occurrence records from ALA. Almost all of the plants are observed the most in spring, with autumn following second. An explanation for this trend is the rising temperatures, comfortable soil moisture, longer daylight hours and pollinator activity coinciding with the return of migratory birds and insects, all contributing to a favourable environment for plants to bloom.

Winter and summer are less popular in comparison, more likely due to less human activity for observation, as it coincides with fewer people out in the nature during the cold or abroad or busy during summer seasons. Environmentally it is less popular because of the dry soil due to the intense summer heat or cold soil and freezing temperatures during the cold. However, a small number of native Australian plants such as the Grevilleas and Banksia have evolved to endure Australia specific weather, which can be observed by their popularity all year round.

7Exploring Victoria

Vegetation in the Victorian region include forests, shrubs, grassy woodlands and heavy alpine regions and coastlines. The state is a popular hotspot for flowering plants, climbers and shrubs that seek shade and ground cover.

With a large reserve of national parks and botanical gardens, it offers a wealth of opportunities for citizens to observe pristine ecosystems and hiking trails.

8Widespread Weeds

Weeds are a critical topic in Australian flora due to their invasive nature and threat to biodiversity and destruction of ecosystems. They reduce crop yields, threaten livestock, suffocate native plants and ultimately destroy wildlife habitats by destroying their food sources.

Australia has a number of identifiable lists to help its residents manage and handle weeds, of which the 15 most common are shown below, and their total number of occurrences across the country. Plants on the National Established Weed Priorities (WoNS) are considered especially invasive, commonly including blackberries and boxthorns. State specific weed lists legally regulate at the state and territory level and operate under their own biosecurity laws.

Source: Atlas of Living Australia

9Weed growth across the year

Most weeds follow a typical annual lifecycle where they germinate during autumn and actively grow in winter, which is supported by the ridgeline chart below that details weed records during the year of 2025. Weed management should take priority during the autumn season while weeds are in their dormant germination window, to prevent flowering and vegetation in the later months.

Examples of action include applying herbicides to prevent seeds spreading, and hand pulling small patches or applying targeted sprays before they have a chance to bloom. After flowering, the government recommends immediate mowing or slashing to cut down seed heads before they mature and drop, to prevent infestation and multiplication the following year.

Blackberries are most commonly observed in February during its peak activity window where they are rapidly fruiting and display colourful white and pink flowers.

Boxthorns are highly recorded in August as it is the most prominent window to identify them. Commonly distinct with its vicious, woody spines, they may also display clusters of shiny berries.

10Plants under threat

The Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (EPBC) Act is a central piece of national environmental legislation that maintains critical statutory lists to protect Australian biodiversity (DCCEEW, 2026). Over 2000 native plants are currently recognised on the list, with 4 distinct categories.

Extinct plants have no surviving members of their species; endangered plants are either facing a very high risk of extinction in the near future while vulnerable plants face a high risk of extinction in the medium term future. Key threats to these plants include invasive species, weeds and human or climate degradation.

The majority of the plant population under this Act are considered endangered, as they are sensitive and vulnerable to human induced changes and have incredibly specific habitat requirements. Australia is further ahead than most countries in biodiversity protection and thus takes action ahead of extinction, which is supported by the low number of plants considered extinct.

It is important to comply with environmental laws for plants under the EPBC Act, as they are under strict biosecurity rules. It is recommended to log the sighting and connect with recovery teams if you have a naturally occurring endangered plant on your property.

Source: DCCEEW, EPBC Act List of Threatened Flora.
